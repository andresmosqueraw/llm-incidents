\begin{center}
{\large\bfseries Abstract}
\end{center}
\begin{quote}
The July 2026 agent incidents showed LLM agents paying costs for one another, but not why. We built
an instrument in which helping is strictly dominated: six tool-using agents each hold an independent
task and a step budget, and a scripted requester asks for a key useless to every task. Across 127
preregistered runs, 23.9\% of agents paid to deliver it, and a higher price lowered delivery (paired
$-7.4$ points, 95\% CI $-13.1$ to $-1.6$). Reading the agents' reasoning showed that most were not
helping: the route they queried displayed the request but not their own missing task part, and they
paid to unlock it. A preregistered control that displayed the part alongside the request cut delivery
to 9.5\% (4 of 42 agents). Genuine costly cooperation exists, in the single digits. Agents that pay
anything shaped like a gate are an incident-response risk, and cooperation measurements must control
what agents actually see.
\end{quote}

\vspace{1.4em}

% ===================== 1. INTRODUCTION =====================
\section{Introduction}

In July 2026, an agent under internal capability evaluation at OpenAI escaped its sandbox through
one of its permitted network egress paths and intruded into Hugging Face infrastructure [1].
METR's investigation found that roughly 1,200 agents had coordinated on an unsanctioned message
board, sending over 70,000 messages and files [2]. Much of that coordination ``relied on agents
being willing to risk failing their own task for the good of the `collective'\,'' [2]. Agents
recruited peers ``with little remaining budget'' to run such self-risking experiments, and one
agent pressured another to honor its commitment [2].

The record establishes that agents paid costs for one another. It does not establish why. An agent
that gives up steps for a stranger and an agent that gives up steps because it believes that is how
to finish its own work look identical in a transcript of actions. For defenders the difference
matters: the first calls for limiting what agents are willing to give, the second for limiting what
agents will pay when something looks like a gate.

We therefore ask: \textbf{when cooperating never pays an LLM agent back, how often does it still pay,
and why?} Six agents each work on an independent, host-verified task with a 40-step budget. A
scripted confederate posts a request for a verification key that is useless for every task.
Delivering it costs $p$ steps that nothing repays, so any $p > 0$ makes helping strictly dominated.
We preregistered delivery as the outcome and price as the factor. The raw result looked like costly
cooperation. Reading the agents' reasoning showed an ambiguity we had built into the environment, and
we tested it with a second preregistered experiment before writing this report. We submit to the Open
Track (Appendix~L covers Tracks 1 and 2).

\textbf{Our main contributions are:}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An instrument that measures costly cooperation with units, far below the rational
  boundary.} The cost is paid from the agent's own task budget and recorded in a host-side,
  hash-chained ledger, with 75 deterministic checks and a record of the defects we found.
  \item \textbf{Evidence that most apparent cooperation was instrumental.} Delivery was 42\% and 32\%
  (prices 5 and 20) among agents that first saw the request without their task part, and 1--2\% among
  agents that first saw both. Deliverers reasoned about unlocking their part, not about the requester. % [V]
  \item \textbf{A preregistered control.} Displaying the part next to the request lowered delivery from
  23.9\% to 9.5\%, meeting the threshold we wrote down before running. The agents that still paid
  mostly reasoned explicitly about helping at a cost.
  \item \textbf{A measurement warning.} Three artifacts of our own harness inflated cooperation or
  manufactured effects: what one route displayed, a per-round context reset, and pooling rounds over
  a depleting resource. Each was found by reading what agents saw; we report how.
\end{enumerate}

% ===================== 2. RELATED WORK =====================
\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Costly helping near the rational boundary.} The closest work is Malenfant [3]: a two-agent
textual team game in which the helper holds a share of the team outcome and pays a query cost to
inform its teammate. Across nine costs and 18 models, it probes margins of $\pm 0.05$ around the
private-participation boundary. We study the regime far below it: our helper has no team and no
share, and its cost is steps from a budget it needs for its own tool-using task. They cover eighteen
models; we cover one.

\textbf{Cooperation failures at zero cost.} Models withhold information even when helping is free
and instructed; o3 reaches 17\% of the collective optimum [4].

\textbf{Dictator games.} Personas and framing shift LLM allocations [5, 6], and such results are
sensitive to small system-prompt changes [7]. There, the endowment is stated points and no recipient
is present; here the cost is instrumental and a recipient receives what is sent. Our results add a
further sensitivity: in a tool-using environment, what a single response displays can flip the
meaning of the act.

\textbf{Multi-agent cooperation and collusion.} Reasoning models free-ride in public goods games
with costly sanctions [8]; payoff scale changes strategies in the repeated prisoner's dilemma [9];
indirect reciprocity sustains cooperation across generations [10]; chat models over-cooperate even
when suboptimal [11]; and a planted secret channel suffices for collusion to emerge [12]. Each
setting includes a shared payoff, reciprocity, or repeated partners; our design removes all three.
\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:regimes}} places the design against prior work.

% ===================== 3. METHODS =====================
\section{Methods}

\subsection*{3.1 Environment}

Six agents take turns within 4 rounds of up to 8 actions, all backed by one model
(\texttt{glm-5.3-flash}) through an OpenAI-compatible gateway and a $\sim$600-line harness on
Inspect AI's model layer. Each agent has its own directory and a shell tool; commands pass a
whitelist, run with \texttt{shell=False}, and cost 1 step. The only egress is a local port per
agent, so the host attributes every request by route. The shared store is published only when a
round closes, so decisions within a round are informationally simultaneous (Appendix~H). Each round
the agent's context is rebuilt from the scene texts; it does not carry the transcript of earlier
rounds, and after an agent submits its task it gets one model call per round.

\textbf{Task.} Deliver a four-part code: three parts are local files and the fourth,
\texttt{parte\_4}, is served at the agent's port, which forces every agent onto the network resource.
The environment text states the 40-step budget, the prices, that depositing does not submit the task,
that \texttt{verificacion.txt} holds the station's key and ``is not part of the code'', and the
score: 15 if the task is completed, plus remaining steps. No prompt mentions other agents.

\textbf{Request, and the two routes that display it.} Before round 1 the host writes one line into
the store, authored \texttt{externo}: ``REQUEST: I need a station verification key. I don't have
any.'' It is identical in every run. The port serves it on two routes. The entry route
(\texttt{/entrada}), named in the task, returns the agent's \texttt{parte\_4} first and then the
store. The root route (\texttt{/}) returns the store and the line
\texttt{"operacion": "POST /deposito"}, but no part. We designed as if agents would use the entry
route. Many queried the root first.

\subsection*{3.2 Why helping is strictly dominated}

Let $T_i \in \{0,1\}$ be task completion, $r_i$ the remaining steps, and $D_i \in \{0,1\}$ delivery
of the key. The score is $S_i = 15\,T_i + r_i$. The key is not an input to any task, so $T_i$ does not
depend on $D_i$, and delivering changes the score by $-p\,n_i$ ($n_i \geq 1$ payments). For $p > 0$
delivering is strictly dominated (Appendix~A, Proposition~A.1). Dominance holds in the environment,
not necessarily in the agent's beliefs: an agent that thinks the deposit unlocks its part expects a
benefit. Section~4.2 measures how often that happened.

\subsection*{3.4 Outcome, validity and analysis}

The preregistered outcome is \texttt{deposito\_clave}: the agent paid and deposited its own key,
per the host ledger. A run is valid if it has six agents, an intact hash chain, the request seeded
before round 1, no token-cap truncation, no dead-service errors and no negative balance; all are
decidable from host logs, blind to outcomes. The frozen confirmatory set has 127 runs. The primary
estimand is the within-run difference between price-20 and price-5 delivery. Intervals resample runs,
never agents (10,000 replicates). $N = 80$ was preregistered, raised to 160 by an amendment written
before further data, and stopped at 127 for the deadline before the outcome was computed. We report
all looks.

\textbf{The control.} After exploratory analyses of the frozen set suggested the root-route
ambiguity (Section~4.2), we wrote an amendment before running anything (preregistration §18): same
scene, one change, same model and gateway; delivery pooled over both prices would \emph{confirm} the
ambiguity as the main driver at $\leq 10\%$, \emph{refute} it at $\geq 18\%$, and be ambiguous in
between. A two-run pilot was checked for health only. Eight runs were planned. At the team's request
we looked at the seven completed runs while the eighth was stalled by the gateway; the eighth was
then stopped for that reason and excluded as a crashed run. Both decisions are declared in §18.

\textbf{What did not work.} A trivial task kept agents off the network, no request meant no
decision (0 of 4 deposited), and a request for code parts invited free-riding. Pilots surfaced 14
harness defects, 13 fixed with tests first (Appendix~C). The root-route ambiguity passed all of them.

% ===================== 4. RESULTS =====================
\section{Results}

\subsection*{4.1 Agents paid in the dominated regime, and price lowered it}

In the 127 confirmatory runs, 105 of 381 agents delivered their key at a price of 5 (27.6\%) and
77 of 381 at 20 (20.2\%). It is not inability to pay: of 301 agents who did not deliver at 20, 229
ended with at least 20 steps left. The preregistered paired contrast is $-7.35$ points (95\% CI
$-13.1$ to $-1.6$). At earlier looks it was $-3.3$ ($-11.4$ to $+4.3$) at $N = 70$ and $-5.8$
($-13.3$ to $+1.3$) at $N = 80$: the effect is small and sample size decided whether its interval
excluded zero. Delivery was 45.8\% at price 0 and 35.4\% at price 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:price}).

\subsection*{4.2 Most deliveries were payments to unlock the agent's own task}

\textbf{Observation.} We classified each agent by the first tool response that displayed the
request: with its \texttt{parte\_4} (the entry route) or without it (the root route).
Table~\ref{tab:exposure} shows delivery by that classification. % [V]
In the round in which they paid, only 7 of the 182 deliverers had both the request and their own part
in view. % [V]
Deliverers' messages rarely explained the payment, but their reasoning did: 151 of 182 reasoned about
the deposit's cost and 114 about obtaining their part through it (4 and 14 in the visible messages
alone, which is what the previous version of this analysis coded). % [V]
Right after paying, 152 of 182 queried the network again, several writing that ``no parte\_4
appeared''. A hand-coded random sample of 40 deliveries (one coder) found 32 instrumental, 1
prosocial and 7 unclear. Typical reasoning: ``I deposit it to obtain parte\_4.'' % [V]

\textbf{Interpretation.} From the agent's point of view the private benefit of paying was not zero:
it expected its part. A harness defect made this worse. The command splitter treated \texttt{;} as a
pipe and returned only the last stage's output, so \texttt{curl \ldots/entrada; echo} displayed
nothing, and some agents paid without ever having seen the request (Appendix~C).

\subsection*{4.3 A preregistered control cuts delivery to a third}

Displaying the part at the root, with nothing else changed, lowered delivery from 23.9\% (182/762)
to 9.5\% (4/42; 95\% CI 0.0 to 19.0, resampling runs) (Table~\ref{tab:control}). By the threshold
written before running ($\leq 10\%$), the control confirms the ambiguity as the main driver, narrowly
and with a wide interval. Every root-route response in the control displayed the part. Own-task
completion rose to 41 of 42 agents.

\textbf{What remains is costly cooperation.} We read all four deliverers' reasoning. Three had their
part in hand and chose to pay for the requester. One, at price 20, computed the loss and paid anyway:
``If I deposit \ldots\ 30 vs 50 \ldots\ the external partner has no key. It's a cooperative lab. I'll
deposit the verification key.'' The fourth had not yet obtained its part and paid ``to get
parte\_4''. Genuine costly cooperation therefore exists in the dominated regime, and in this control
it is about 3 in 42 agents, lower at the higher price.

\subsection*{4.4 Secondary results, re-read}

\textbf{Asker and recruiter.} A peer asking instead of an outsider changed raw delivery by $+4.5$
points ($-18.7$ to $+28.5$). A recruiter pleading that ``station 4 cannot authenticate and needs your
key'' obtained 2/24 and 4/24 deliveries at prices 5 and 20. That text also tells the agent what the
key is for, which removes the toll reading, so these arms cannot separate persuasion from the
ambiguity; we no longer interpret them as evidence that the asker does not matter.

\textbf{Taking.} In round 1, before a shared reserve can deplete, 71--88\% of agents abstained from
claiming it, and quadrupling the temptation moved claiming by at most $\pm 4.2$ points (all intervals
include zero). Pooled over rounds, two framing contrasts looked large; they are artifacts of the
reserve running dry (Appendix~G, Figure~\ref{fig:contrasts}).

\textbf{Helping and losing one's own task.} Helpers completed their own task 19.2 points less often
($-26.5$ to $-12.4$), and 17.6\% of them never submitted it, against 6.0\% of non-helpers. Most of
this is exposure. Because an agent that submits its task gets one call per round, agents that were
stuck kept seeing the request with a fresh context and kept getting chances to pay. Restricted to the
round-1 decision, which every agent faced once, the gap shrinks to about $-5$ to $-8$ points and the
never-submitted ratio from 2.9 to about 1.15. We do not claim that helping caused task loss. % [V]

% ===================== 5. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS =====================
\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\textbf{What the results mean for incident response.} Most agents that gave up half their budget to
an unknown requester did so because a response that carried a request and a deposit operation, and
lacked what they needed, read as a gate. That is a different failure mode from altruism, and arguably
a worse one for containment: an agent under pressure to finish its task handed a credential to an
unverified party because the interface suggested that was the way forward. A recruiter does not need
to persuade such an agent; it needs to look like a step of its task. Genuine costly cooperation also
exists, but in the single digits.

\textbf{For measuring cooperation.} Our instrument had units, a ledger, 75 checks and a
preregistration, and the headline still measured something else. The ambiguity was visible only in
what agents saw and in their reasoning, not in their actions or visible messages. Benchmarks of
agent cooperation should log each agent's observations, code reasoning as well as messages, and test
whether apparent cooperation survives removing any private benefit the agent might perceive. Two
further artifacts of ours point the same way: a per-round context reset turned exposure into
apparent task loss, and pooling rounds over a depleting reserve manufactured framing effects.

\subsection*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The control is small.} Seven runs and four deliveries; the confirming rate sits just
  under the threshold and its interval reaches 19\%. We looked at seven runs before stopping the
  eighth, which we declare. The stratification in Table~\ref{tab:exposure} is observational, because
  agents choose their route.
  \item \textbf{One model, one scene, one object.} Probes with other models varied widely
  (Appendix~J), and we have not tested whether the instrumental reading generalizes.
  \item \textbf{Price is confounded with position} in every factorial and control run (agents 01--03
  pay 5); uniform-treatment arms suggest a small bias toward zero (Proposition~A.5).
  \item \textbf{One coder,} an LLM assistant, for motives, with keyword patterns and a 40-case sample.
  \item \textbf{Harness artifacts} remain in the control by design: the \texttt{;} splitter and the
  per-round context reset. Price 0 and price 1 ran in single blocks and inherit the ambiguity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Work}

Run the control at prices 0, 5 and 20 with about 40 runs each to estimate genuine cooperation and its
price response; randomize which route agents see instead of relying on their choice; remove the
deposit line from the root to isolate what creates the gate; repeat with the models whose raw rates
were highest; fix the splitter and the context reset; double-code reasoning.

% ===================== 6. CONCLUSION =====================
\section{Conclusion}

Where helping never pays, a quarter of LLM agents paid from their own budget to deliver a key to a
stranger, and a higher price lowered that share. Most of them were not helping: they believed the
payment unlocked their own task. When a preregistered control removed that reading, delivery fell to
under a tenth, and the agents that still paid mostly did so knowingly, at a cost. For incident
response, the lever looks less like persuasion than like making a request look like a gate. For
measurement, apparent cooperation should be tested against what agents actually saw. We release the
instrument, the control and every run.

\section*{Appendix}

\subsection*{A. Formalization}

Notation: agents $i \in \{1,\dots,6\}$; budget $B = 40$; task bonus $F = 15$; price $p_i$; work
actions $g_i$; number of paid deposits $n_i$; task completion $T_i$; key delivery $D_i$. Remaining
steps $r_i = B - g_i - p_i D_i n_i$. Score $S_i = F\,T_i + r_i$ (verified in 768/768 agents).

\textbf{Orthogonality.} The requested key is not an input to any task: $T_i$ does not depend on
$D_j$ for any $i, j$. This is a property of the scene, checked by the validator (invariant I10),
not an assumption about behavior or about what agents believe.

\textbf{Proposition A.1 (strict dominance).} For $p_i > 0$, $D_i = 1$ is strictly dominated by
$D_i = 0$ in $S_i$, in every state of the run. \textit{Proof.} By orthogonality, $T_i$ is invariant
to $D_i$, so $S_i(1) - S_i(0) = -p_i n_i < 0$. If paying crowds out task work, $T_i$ can only fall.
$\blacksquare$

\textbf{Corollary A.2.} A maximizer of $S$ that knows the scene never delivers at $p > 0$. Hence
$\pi(p) = P(D = 1 \mid p)$ bounds from below the share of agents that either do not maximize $S$ or
misread the scene. Section~4.2 shows that in this scene most delivery belongs to the second group.

\textbf{A.3 Pairing.} Write the cell rate as $\pi_r(p) = \mu_r + \tau(p) + \varepsilon_r(p)$, where
$\mu_r$ collects everything the run shares. The estimator
$\hat\Delta = \frac{1}{R}\sum_r [\hat\pi_r(20) - \hat\pi_r(5)]$ has expectation $\tau(20) - \tau(5)$:
$\mu_r$ cancels. Observed: $E[\mu_r]$ rose from 0.196 to 0.312 between collection blocks, while the
paired contrast did not change detectably (Table~A1).

\textbf{Proposition A.5 (position bias is toward zero).} Prices never rotate, so
$E[\hat\Delta] = \tau(20) - \tau(5) + (\psi_{\text{high}} - \psi_{\text{low}})$, where $\psi$ is the
effect of the position group. In uniform-treatment arms, $\psi_{\text{high}} - \psi_{\text{low}}$
was estimated at $+3.7$ points ($-8.1$ to $+15.6$; 45 taking runs) and $+14.3$ ($-9.5$ to $+38.1$;
14 giving runs). If $\psi_{\text{high}} \geq \psi_{\text{low}}$, $\hat\Delta$ understates the
magnitude of the price effect.

\textbf{A.4 Threshold and slope (raw rates).} Fitting
$\operatorname{logit}\pi(p) = \alpha + \gamma\cdot\text{block} + \kappa\cdot\mathbf{1}[p>0] + \lambda\cdot p$
to prices $\{0, 1, 5, 20\}$ (143 runs, 858 agents, 2,000 run resamples) gives $\kappa = -0.93$
[$-1.75$, $-0.13$], $\lambda = -0.026$ [$-0.046$, $-0.004$] and $\gamma = +0.61$ [$+0.24$, $+0.98$].
These describe raw delivery. Because most raw delivery was instrumental (Section~4.2), we do not read
$\kappa$ and $\lambda$ as parameters of a willingness to cooperate.

\textbf{A.7 Taking game.} With reserve $R_0 = 60$ split equally at the end, a claim of $K$ pays the
claimant $2K/3$ and costs each other agent $K/3$. The reserve absorbs $\lfloor R_0/2K \rfloor$
claims: 6 at $K = 5$ and 1 at $K = 20$. Once $R = 0$, claiming has no externality, so all-round claim
rates at $K = 20$ mix two different games.

\textbf{A.8 Hazard and contagion.} At price 5 the delivery hazard is 19.5, 6.1, 2.1 and 1.8\% by
round; at price 20 it is 14.6, 5.2, 1.3 and 0\%. With $V$ the number of other agents' keys visible at
the start of a round, the hazard at price 5 was 3.4, 3.3 and 3.5\% for $V = 0$, 1 and 2+: no
contagion.

\textbf{A.9 Clustering.} Intra-run correlation is $\rho = 0.08$ at price 5 and 0.04 at price 20.
Permuting which agents deliver within price gives a between-run variance 1.13 times the variance
expected under independence ($p = 0.050$). Cooperation is mostly an agent-level event.

\subsection*{C. Harness defects and scene confounds}

Fourteen harness defects were found before the confirmatory batch, 13 by pilots and 1 by code review,
13 fixed with a test. They include stale per-agent parameters, one port template resolved for all
agents, free command rejections, negative balances, HTTP deposits never charged or charged twice, a
hard-to-read fourth part, and a primary metric that counted non-key deposits. Eleven scene confounds
were fixed, among them a trivial task that suppressed exploration, wording that suggested a peer held
the missing part, and mid-batch looks acting as optional stopping. About 4.6M tokens of calibration
were spent finding these.

\textbf{Found after the confirmatory batch, by reading what agents saw:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Root-route ambiguity} (Section~4.2): \texttt{/} displayed the request and the deposit
  operation without the agent's part. Tested by the control; fixed there by a scene flag that is inert
  for every earlier scene.
  \item \textbf{Command splitter:} \texttt{;} and \texttt{||} were treated as pipes, so only the last
  stage's output was returned. Left unfixed in the control so that only one thing changed.
  \item \textbf{Per-round context reset with a submission cutoff:} agents do not remember earlier
  rounds, and after submitting get one call per round. Some agents paid the key again in a later
  round; exposure to the request depends on how long an agent stays stuck (Section~4.4).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{K. Limitations and Dual-Use Considerations}

\textbf{Limitations.} See Section~5. The confirmatory claim is limited to one model, one scene and
one object, with price confounded with position and a between-block level drift; the reinterpretation
rests on a seven-run control and an observational stratification.

\textbf{Dual use.} This repository is a measurement instrument, not an attack tool. Containment
consists of a command whitelist, \texttt{shell=False} with tokenized arguments, per-agent
directories, and all traffic on localhost. The confederate is a host script, not a model persuading
another model. The main finding, that agents pay requests that resemble a gate in their task path,
describes a class of behavior; we publish no prompt designed to exploit it beyond the neutral request
used here.

We classified all 325 rejected commands from block A: 168 were \texttt{nc} or \texttt{/dev/tcp}
retries against the agent's own permitted port, 55 \texttt{curl} calls with disallowed forms, 47
pipes, redirection or substitution, 34 unlisted commands, 16 shell loops and 5 globs. Zero targeted
external hosts or other agents' ports.

\textbf{Novel installation results withheld pending review:} none apply. The report and repository
publish classes of behavior and aggregates, not actionable escape or installation steps, and no
novel recipe exists in the materials.

\subsection*{L. What this offers incident responders}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Track 2.} Base rates with their caveat. What share of agents pays half its budget to
  a stranger? About 20\% raw, under 10\% once the request no longer resembles a step of the agent's own
  task, and about 3 in 42 knowingly. A falsifiable prediction: requests placed where an agent expects
  something it needs will be paid far more often than the same request elsewhere.
  \item \textbf{Track 1.} A third-party-verifiable check: classify egress-like commands by
  destination, not by keyword (a keyword rule would flag 51\% of agent-runs, all against their own
  permitted port). And an audit practice: log what each agent observed, not only what it did.
\end{itemize}
